Tokens & Tokenizationthe boundary where your data layer meets the model

A model cannot read the letter f. It can only multiply numbers. Tokenization is the translation step that turns text into integers — and almost every surprising cost, context, and accuracy problem you will hit starts here.

Why subwords, not words

A word-per-ID dictionary breaks three ways: the vocabulary is unbounded, anything unseen becomes <UNK> and is destroyed, and fraud / fraudulent / defraud become unrelated IDs that share nothing.

Subword tokenization sits in the middle: common words stay whole, rare words fragment. Note in the simulator that the leading space is part of the token —  fraud and fraud are different tokens. That trips people up constantly.

Rule of thumbValue
English prose~4 characters ≈ 1 token · ~0.75 words ≈ 1 token
100 words of English~133 tokens · a full page ≈ 500 tokens
CodeDenser — punctuation and indentation each cost tokens
Devanagari (Marathi / Hindi)3–5× more tokens for the same meaning
Hex identifiers, UUIDs, base58Near worst case — roughly 1 token per 1–2 characters

The wallet-address problem

Run the Wallet address preset. A 42-character Ethereum address shreds into ~20–25 meaningless fragments. Three consequences for your fraud work:

  • Cost. Fifty addresses in a prompt burns over a thousand tokens on noise.
  • No semantics. Two addresses differing by one character produce near-identical token sequences. The model may treat them as related — exactly wrong in a fraud context.
  • Bad recall. Echoing an address back means reproducing ~24 arbitrary tokens in exact order. It will hallucinate characters.

The fix is a data decision, not a prompt decision: pass surrogate keys and features, keep real addresses in the lakehouse.

bad "Is 0x3f5CE5FBFe3E9af3971dD833D26bA9b5C936f0bE suspicious?" good "wallet_A: age_days=3, in_degree=412, mixer_hops=1, risk=0.87 — assess." wallet_A resolves to the real address outside the model, from dim_wallet

Interview point

"Tokenization is the boundary between the data layer and the model layer, and most production LLM failures start there. On-chain identifiers tokenize into ~20 meaningless pieces each, so I normalise them to stable surrogate keys upstream and keep the real addresses in the lakehouse. That's dimensional modelling logic applied to a prompt."

Why LLMs fumble arithmetic

Numbers tokenize inconsistently — 2024 may be one token while 1379 splits into 13+79. The model never sees clean place-value columns. That's why the fix is a calculator tool, not more training.

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What is a token?

A subword piece from a fixed vocabulary — the unit the model actually reads. ~4 characters of English. Not a word.

Why subwords instead of words?

Fixed vocabulary size, zero unknown-word failures, and shared roots share representation.

Why are LLMs bad at multi-digit maths?

Digits tokenize inconsistently, so place value is destroyed at the input. Give it a tool instead.

Is the context window measured in words?

No — tokens. A 200k window is roughly 150k English words, far fewer in Marathi.

Can you extend a trained model's vocabulary?

Not cheaply. The tokenizer is frozen before training; new tokens mean new embedding rows and retraining.

Nobody hand-writes the token list. BPE starts from raw bytes — so nothing is ever unrepresentable — then repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair. Run it below and watch fraud and tion emerge on their own.

What to watch: the algorithm has no idea what a word is. It only counts adjacent pairs. Morphology — fraud, tion, s — falls out of frequency alone. That is the entire trick.

The algorithm in four lines

  1. Start with every byte as a token (~256 symbols). Nothing is unrepresentable — any language, emoji, or binary garbage encodes.
  2. Count every adjacent pair across a huge corpus.
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into one new token. Add it to the vocabulary.
  4. Repeat 50,000–200,000 times.

After enough merges you have discovered the, ing, tion,  transaction, (); — without a single linguistic rule.

Interview point

"BPE is a compression algorithm repurposed as a vocabulary learner. Because it bottoms out at bytes, the tokenizer has a zero-unknown-token guarantee — which is why the failure mode isn't a crash, it's silent inefficiency on out-of-distribution text like hashes or non-Latin scripts."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
How does BPE choose merges?

Purely by adjacency frequency in the training corpus. Greedy, no linguistics.

Why start from bytes rather than characters?

Bytes cover every possible input — no unknown tokens, any script or binary.

Vocabulary size trade-off?

Bigger vocab = shorter sequences but a larger embedding and output matrix. Typical range 32k–200k.

A single learned number inside the model, mostly held in the feed-forward matrices.

Do more parameters mean a better model?

Directionally, with heavy diminishing returns. Data quality and training-token count often beat raw size.

Loss curve — cross-entropy per step. Push the learning rate past ~2 and watch it oscillate; drop it to 0.02 and watch it crawl. That one dial is the difference between a model that trains and one that doesn't.

The loop, in five lines

  1. Forward pass. Feed the inputs in, get a prediction out.
  2. Loss. Compare prediction to truth. One number: how wrong.
  3. Backpropagation. Compute, for every parameter, the derivative of the loss with respect to it — "which way should this knob move, and how much does it matter?"
  4. Update. Nudge every parameter a small step down that slope. Step size = learning rate.
  5. Repeat.

You are standing on a foggy hill in a space with as many dimensions as parameters, trying to reach the lowest valley. You can't see anything, but you can feel the slope under your feet. The fog is why it takes months and thousands of GPUs.

Note what training is not: nobody wrote a rule about grammar or fraud. The parameters encoding "verbs follow subjects" emerged purely from prediction error being minimised over trillions of tokens.

Interview point

"Learning rate has the worst failure mode of any hyperparameter — too high and the loss diverges silently mid-run, too low and you burn budget going nowhere. Same shape of problem as tuning Spark shuffle partitions: no universal right answer, and you find it by instrumenting the run rather than guessing."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What does backpropagation compute?

The gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter — direction and magnitude of the fix.

What is cross-entropy loss?

How surprised the model was by the correct answer. Confident and right → near 0; confident and wrong → large.

What is perplexity?

exp(loss). Reads as "how many options was the model effectively choosing between." Comparable only on the same dataset.

Why does learning rate matter so much?

Too high diverges, too low never converges within budget. First thing to check on a failed run.

Token embeddings vs sentence embeddings

People conflate these constantly, and it matters:

  • Token embeddings live inside the model. One per token, and they get transformed at every layer. This is what the transformer actually operates on.
  • Sentence / document embeddings come from a separate purpose-built model. Feed in a chunk of text, get back one vector representing the whole chunk. This is what powers search and RAG.

Two rules people break

Query and documents must use the same embedding model. Vectors from different models live in different spaces — comparing them gives noise that looks like results.

Change the embedding model → re-embed everything. It's a full backfill, not a config change. Treat the embedding model as a schema version.

The known weakness

Embeddings capture topic, not logic. "The transaction was flagged" and "the transaction was not flagged" sit almost on top of each other — same subject matter. Negation, numbers, and dates are systematically weak. That's the entire argument for hybrid retrieval, next module.

Interview point

"Embeddings turn semantic similarity into a geometry problem, which makes retrieval quality an ETL problem. I version the embedding model as part of the table schema — swapping it is a backfill. Most RAG systems degrade silently because nobody treated it that way."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What is an embedding?

A learned vector for a token or chunk, where semantic similarity equals geometric closeness.

Why cosine and not Euclidean distance?

Angle captures direction/meaning; magnitude mostly tracks frequency.

Can you mix embedding models between indexing and querying?

No. Different vector spaces. Changing the model means a full re-embed.

What do embeddings handle badly?

Negation, exact numbers, dates, and identifiers. Cover those with keyword search.

Why hybrid, concretely

Try each preset in all three modes and you'll see the failure pattern:

QueryDenseBM25
"money laundering patterns"Wins — surfaces the placement and layering documents despite sharing no words with the queryTotal miss — zero matches, returns nothing

Reciprocal Rank Fusion combines them without needing the two score scales to be comparable: score = Σ 1/(k + rank), k≈60. It only uses rank position, which is why it's robust and why it's the default choice.

The RAG pipeline is a data pipeline

  1. Chunk the corpus — the single most under-rated decision. Too big and retrieval is imprecise; too small and context is lost. Respect document structure over fixed character counts.
  2. Embed each chunk, store vectors plus metadata (source, date, jurisdiction, entity).
  3. Retrieve top-k at query time, with metadata filters first — a semantically perfect but out-of-date regulation is a wrong answer.
  4. Rerank (optional, high value) with a cross-encoder over the top ~50.
  5. Generate with the retrieved chunks in the prompt and a demand for citations.

Every one of those steps is freshness, partitioning, schema, and quality — the work you already do. You don't need a dedicated vector database to start; Postgres with pgvector, or an Iceberg table plus an index, is often enough.

Interview point

"RAG quality is dominated by chunking, metadata filters, and corpus freshness — not prompt wording. In my fraud copilot, retrieval is a partitioned lakehouse read with a recency filter before the vector search ever runs, because a stale typology retrieved perfectly is still a wrong answer."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What is RAG in one line?

Retrieve relevant chunks at query time and put them in the prompt, so the answer is grounded in real sources.

Why hybrid over pure vector search?

Dense is weak on exact IDs, numbers, and negation; BM25 covers those. Fuse ranks with RRF.

What is RRF?

Reciprocal Rank Fusion — combines rankings using 1/(k+rank), so incomparable score scales don't matter.

Biggest lever on RAG quality?

Chunking strategy and metadata filtering, then reranking. Prompt wording is last.

Do you need a vector database?

Not at first. pgvector or a lakehouse table with an index works until scale or latency forces the move.

Temperature as a spice dial

Mechanically it divides every logit by T before softmax. Low T sharpens the distribution, high T flattens it and makes junk tokens viable.

TaskTemperature
Extraction, classification, JSON output0
Code generation0 – 0.3
Analysis, summarisation0.3 – 0.7
Creative writing, brainstorming0.8 – 1.2

Anything in a fraud pipeline that produces a structured artefact runs at T = 0. You don't want creativity in a compliance record.

T = 0 is not truly deterministic

Floating-point non-associativity and variable GPU batching mean identical prompts can occasionally diverge. Never design a system that requires bit-identical model output — validate the shape instead.

Why hallucination is structural

Look at what actually happens: the model samples from a probability distribution over next tokens. There is no truth check anywhere in the pipeline. Fluency and accuracy are produced by the same mechanism. If the training data has few examples of "I don't know," then a confident answer is simply the high-probability continuation.

Which reframes the fix. You don't prompt hallucination away — you ground (retrieve real sources), constrain (schema-validate the output), and verify (check claims against a system of record). All three are data-engineering controls.

Interview point

"Hallucination isn't a bug, it's the sampling process working as designed — nothing in the forward pass distinguishes true from plausible. So I treat it like data quality and constrain at the boundary: retrieval for grounding, schemas for shape, a validation gate before anything reaches a downstream table. Write-Audit-Publish, applied to model output."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What is a logit?

A raw unbounded score per vocabulary entry, before softmax turns it into a probability.

What does temperature do mechanically?

Divides logits before softmax. Low sharpens the distribution, high flattens it.

Why do models hallucinate?

Sampling optimises for plausible continuations; nothing checks truth. Fix by grounding and validation, not prompting.

Temperature for structured extraction?

Zero, plus a schema-constrained output mode. Never rely on prose instructions for JSON shape.

What the loop explains

  • Output costs more than input. Prompt tokens are processed in parallel; output tokens strictly one at a time.
  • Streaming looks word-by-word because that is literally the generation rate.
  • The model can't plan or revise. It only ever chooses the next token and can never take one back. Chain-of-thought works precisely because it turns "plan then answer" into more next-token predictions.

Interview point

"A 500-token answer is 500 complete forward passes through every parameter. That asymmetry drives the whole cost model — design prompts to be long and answers short, not the reverse, and never ask for restated context in the output."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
Top-k vs top-p?

Fixed count vs adaptive probability mass. Top-p adapts to model confidence, so it's the usual default.

Why is output more expensive than input?

Prefill is parallel; decode is sequential — one full forward pass per generated token.

Why does chain-of-thought help?

It converts reasoning into more next-token predictions, giving the model intermediate state it can condition on.

Can a model revise what it already wrote?

Not within one generation. Only by re-running with the previous output in context.

Context window & the KV cachewhy long prompts are expensive, and why memory is the real wall

The context window is not memory. Nothing persists between calls — a chat feels continuous only because the client resends the entire history every turn. And the cost of that grows in a very unfriendly way.

KV cache = 2 (K and V) × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × tokens × 2 bytes × users. It is per request and grows linearly with context — which is why long contexts limit concurrency. Memory, not compute, is usually the wall. Grouped-query attention (few KV heads, many query heads) exists almost entirely to shrink this number.

The quadratic problem

In attention every token compares itself to every other token. n tokens → n² comparisons.

TokensComparisons
1,0001 million
10,000100 million
100,00010 billion

10× the context is 100× the attention compute. That single fact drives most architecture research of the last few years.

Prompt caching — order your prompt like a partition key

Providers can persist the KV cache for a fixed prefix. Put your stable system prompt and reference documents at the front and vary only the tail, and you get large cost and latency reductions. Prompt ordering is now a performance optimisation — the same instinct as column ordering and partition pruning.

Lost in the middle

Big context ≠ used context. Models reliably attend best to the beginning and end of a long prompt and degrade in the middle. Put instructions at the top and the most critical evidence at top or bottom. "It fits in the window" and "it's actually being attended to" are different claims.

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
Is the context window memory?

No. It's per-request working state. Continuity comes from the client resending history.

What is the KV cache?

Cached Key/Value vectors so each new token doesn't recompute history. Grows linearly with context, per request.

Why does attention scale badly?

Every token attends to every token — n² in sequence length.

What is grouped-query attention for?

Fewer KV heads than query heads, which shrinks the KV cache and raises achievable concurrency.

How do you exploit prompt caching?

Keep a long stable prefix and vary only the tail. Ordering the prompt becomes a cost decision.

The mistake that gets made every time

"We'll fine-tune the model on our documents so it knows them." That gives you a model which mimics the style of your documents while still inventing details — and you retrain every time a document changes. Facts that move belong in retrieval.

For a fraud copilot: RAG for typologies, regulations, and case history — they change, they need citation, and an auditor will ask where a claim came from. LoRA only if you need an output format or analyst voice that prompting can't reliably hold.

You get style mimicry plus invented details, and a retrain on every document change. Retrieve instead.

When is continued pretraining justified?

A genuinely under-represented domain or language, with enough tokens and budget to matter.

What must you build before any fine-tune?

An eval set. Otherwise you cannot tell whether the fine-tune helped or quietly broke something.

LoRA & QLoRAfreeze the expensive thing, version the cheap delta

Full fine-tuning updates all seven billion parameters and hands you a 14GB copy per task. LoRA freezes every original weight and trains small injected matrices instead — often under 1% of the total. Size one below.

The maths: a d×d weight matrix is replaced by an update B·A where A is r×d and B is d×r. Params drop from d² to 2·r·d. At d=4096, r=16 that's 16.7M → 131k per matrix, a 128× reduction. Rank is capacity: r=8–16 for style and format, r=64+ for genuinely new tasks.

QLoRA in one line

The same, but the frozen base is also quantised to 4-bit — so a 7B fine-tune fits on a single consumer GPU. It's the reason hobbyist fine-tuning exists at all.

Interview point

"Think of the base model as a shared upstream table you can't modify and LoRA as a thin incremental layer on top. You keep one copy of the expensive thing and version the cheap deltas — and you can hot-swap adapters per task at serve time. That's the same reason we don't rewrite a fact table to add a derived column."

Catastrophic forgetting

Fine-tuning on a narrow dataset can degrade unrelated abilities the model previously had. Always run a general regression eval alongside your task eval, not just the metric you're optimising.

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What does LoRA freeze?

All original weights. It trains small injected low-rank matrices, typically under 1% of parameters.

What does rank r control?

Adapter capacity. Low r for style and format; higher r for new tasks. Higher r costs more and overfits faster.

QLoRA vs LoRA?

QLoRA also quantises the frozen base to 4-bit, cutting memory enough for single-GPU fine-tuning.

What is catastrophic forgetting?

Fine-tuning degrades unrelated abilities. Guard with a general regression eval.

What does MoE change about parameter counts?

Does MoE reduce memory?

What is the load-balancing loss for?

To stop the router collapsing onto a few favourite experts and wasting the rest of the capacity.

Explain an LLM in one paragraph.

Text is split into subword tokens, each mapped to a learned vector. Those vectors pass through stacked transformer blocks where attention mixes information between positions and feed-forward layers process each position. The final vector becomes a probability distribution over the vocabulary; one token is sampled, appended, and the whole thing runs again. Every parameter was learned by minimising next-token prediction error.

Output tokens. Prefill is parallel and cheap; decode is one full forward pass per token. Design prompts long and answers short.

Usually the KV cache, not the weights. It grows linearly with context and is per-request — which is why GQA and prompt caching exist.

Sampling optimises for plausible continuations and nothing in the forward pass checks truth.

Grounding via retrieval, constrained decoding plus schema validation, and an explicit "not in the sources" branch you can count.

The Transformer

Attention, mechanicallyQ, K, V is a retrieval operation — you already built one in module 06

Every token emits a query ("what am I looking for?"), advertises a key ("what am I?"), and carries a value ("what do I contribute?"). Score every query against every key, softmax the scores, and take a weighted average of the values. That is the whole mechanism.

The four steps

  1. Project. Each token vector is multiplied by three learned matrices to produce Q, K, V. Same input, three different views of it.
  2. Score. score(i,j) = qᵢ · kⱼ / √d. The dot product is similarity; the √d divisor stops large dimensions from saturating softmax into a one-hot spike.
  3. Normalise. Softmax across each row, so every token's attention over the sequence sums to 1. This is the same softmax from module 07 — attention is a probability distribution too.
  4. Mix. Output is the weighted average of the value vectors. A token's new representation is literally a blend of the tokens it decided mattered.

The framing that lands in interviews

"Attention is soft dictionary lookup. Keys are what's indexed, queries are what's asked, values are what's returned — except instead of retrieving one row you retrieve a weighted blend of all of them. It's the same shape as the retrieval layer in a RAG pipeline, run once per token per layer, with the similarity function learned rather than cosine."

Causal masking

Toggle it in the simulator. A generative model must not see the future — during training the whole sentence is present at once, so positions after the current one are set to −∞ before softmax, which drives their weight to zero. That single mask is what makes parallel training possible while keeping generation honest: every position is trained to predict the next token using only what precedes it.

Read the simulator carefully

These projection matrices are hand-set so each head's role is legible. Real heads learn their own projections and their roles are messier and often uninterpretable. What is real here is the arithmetic — dot product, scale, mask, softmax, weighted sum.

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What are Q, K and V?

Three learned projections of the same token vector: what it seeks, what it offers as a match target, and what it contributes if matched.

Why divide by √d?

Dot products grow with dimension. Without the scale, softmax saturates and gradients vanish.

What does the causal mask do?

Sets future positions to −∞ before softmax so they get zero weight — lets you train on a whole sequence in parallel without leaking the answer.

Why is attention O(n²)?

Every query is scored against every key. n tokens → n² scores, per head, per layer.

Self-attention vs cross-attention?

Self: Q, K, V all from the same sequence. Cross: queries from one sequence, keys and values from another — how a decoder reads an encoder.

One softmax can express one relationship. Parallel heads with different projections let a layer model several at once.

Attention is permutation-invariant. Without position, word order carries no information.

Grouped-query attention: many query heads share fewer key/value heads. Shrinks the KV cache with minimal quality loss.

What are the two sublayers of a transformer block?

Multi-head self-attention, then a position-wise feed-forward network. Each wrapped in a residual and a norm.

What does the FFN do that attention can't?

Per-token nonlinear processing. Attention only mixes; the FFN transforms — and holds roughly two-thirds of the parameters.

Each block learns a correction, and gradients flow back through the addition — depth becomes trainable.

The paper used post-norm. Pre-norm is far more stable at depth and is now standard.

Post-norm, sinusoidal positions, ReLU FFN — plus full multi-head KV and the encoder-decoder shape for generative use.

The strategies, by effort

  • Fixed size — cut every N tokens. Fast, structure-blind. A baseline to measure against, never a final answer.
  • Recursive — try paragraph breaks, then sentences, then words. The sensible default and what most libraries do.
  • Structure-aware — split on the document's own boundaries: headers, sections, regulation clauses, functions in a file. Usually the single biggest improvement, because your documents already contain human-authored semantics that fixed-size splitting throws away.
  • Semantic — embed each sentence and cut where consecutive similarity drops. Expensive at index time; occasionally worth it on unstructured prose.

The two techniques that matter most

Contextual chunking. Before embedding, prepend a one-line generated description of where the chunk sits in its parent document. One cheap call per chunk at index time, and it fixes self-referential chunks at the root. Toggle it in the simulator.

Small-to-big retrieval. Embed small precise chunks for search, but return the parent section to the model. Precision in the search, completeness in the context, very little code.

The four techniques

  • Query rewriting. Turn a conversational fragment into a standalone question using the history. Mandatory for chat over RAG — skipping it is the most common cause of "it worked in testing but not with real users."
  • Multi-query expansion. Generate 3–5 paraphrases, retrieve for each, fuse with RRF. Catches vocabulary mismatch between user and corpus.

The four rules

  • Order deliberately. Models attend best to the start and end of a long prompt. Put the strongest evidence at an edge, never buried mid-stack.
  • Deduplicate. Overlapping chunks from the same section waste tokens and over-weight one source by sheer repetition.

Design rules

  • Reasoning fields first. Generation is left-to-right and irreversible — a field emitted earlier conditions everything after it. Put risk_score first and the model commits to a number before reasoning, then rationalises backwards.
  • Enums for anything categorical. Enforceable; a free string is not.
  • Always include an escape branch. Without an insufficient_evidence option, a constrained schema forces fabrication. You have made hallucination mandatory.
  • Flat beats deeply nested. Deep nesting raises grammar complexity, slows first token, and degrades quality.
  • Constrain shape, not thought. Leave the reasoning field free-text.

Still validate afterwards. Constrained decoding guarantees syntax, never semantics. It cannot know whether 0.87 is the right score or whether the cited source exists. The validator from the guardrails module stays — it just moves from checking shape to checking meaning.

Interview point

"Structured output should be a hard guarantee, not a prompt hope — constrained decoding masks non-conforming tokens to −∞ before softmax, so malformed output is unreachable rather than unlikely. Quick-fire · night-before sheet
How does constrained decoding work?

A state machine tracks the schema and masks non-conforming tokens to −∞ before softmax. Their probability is exactly zero.

JSON mode vs structured outputs?

JSON mode guarantees syntax only. Schema-constrained decoding guarantees your fields, types, and enums.

Why does field order matter?

Generation is left-to-right and irreversible. Reasoning before conclusions, or the model rationalises backwards.

Danger of over-constraining?

No escape branch means the schema forces fabrication — hallucination becomes mandatory.

Does it remove the need to validate?

No. Syntax is guaranteed, semantics never. Range, enum, and cross-field checks stay.

What does it cost?

Grammar compilation on TTFT (cache it), and some quality loss if you over-constrain reasoning fields.

The error that silently ruins everything

The chat template used in training must byte-for-byte match the one used at inference — special tokens, role markers, whitespace, where the assistant turn begins. Mismatch it and you get a model that appears to have learned nothing, with no error anywhere. Use the tokenizer's apply_chat_template on both sides. Related: mask the prompt tokens in the loss so gradient signal lands on the response, not the question.

Reading the run

  • Train loss down, validation loss up → overfitting. Fewer epochs, lower rank, or more data.
  • Both flat → learning rate too low, or target_modules names don't match this architecture, so the adapter is attached to nothing.
  • Spikes or NaN → learning rate too high, or extreme-length examples.
  • Loss perfect, outputs wrong → template mismatch, or you trained on the prompt as well as the response.

Loss going down is not success. It only means the model reproduces your training data better. Success is the held-out set, against the prompted baseline.

Three evals, always

  1. Task eval — the held-out set on the thing you're optimising.
  2. Regression eval — general capability unrelated to your task, to catch catastrophic forgetting.
  3. The baseline — the prompted base model with few-shot examples. If you can't beat it, ship the prompt.

Three production concerns

  • Authorisation happens in your code. Always against the user's permissions, never against arguments the model supplied. An agent reading untrusted case notes can be talked into proposing anything — fine, as long as proposing isn't doing. Irreversible actions get a human gate.

  • Freshness. A cached million-token prefix is stale the moment the corpus changes. High churn destroys the cache economics.
  • Attribution. "Which document said this?" is trivial when you retrieved five chunks and near-impossible when you pasted everything.

Where long context genuinely wins: a single large document that must be reasoned over as a whole — a contract, a codebase, one case file — where chunking would destroy the cross-references that matter. The mature answer is not either/or: retrieve to narrow, then use a long window to reason over what you retrieved.

Multimodal, briefly

Images, audio, and video enter the same stream as tokens. An encoder turns a patch of an image into vectors that sit in the same space as text embeddings, and from the transformer's point of view nothing has changed — it is still attending over a sequence. Two practical consequences: images are expensive (a high-resolution image can cost over a thousand tokens, and detail settings are a real cost lever), and OCR-style extraction is now a model call, which for scanned compliance documents is often the whole ingestion pipeline.

Interview point

"Long context doesn't replace retrieval, it changes where the boundary sits. Stuffing pays for the entire corpus on every call and goes stale the moment anything changes, so at production volume it loses on cost, latency, freshness, and attribution. What I'd actually build is retrieve-then-reason: narrow with retrieval, then give the model a generous window over the surviving evidence."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
Does a 1M-token window make RAG obsolete?

No. Retrieval still wins on cost, latency, freshness, and attribution at production volume.

When is long context the right answer?

One large document reasoned over as a whole, where chunking would break the cross-references that matter.

What is "lost in the middle"?

Recall degrades for facts placed mid-context. Put critical evidence at an edge.

What made long context viable?

Mostly positional work — RoPE with frequency scaling — plus attention and KV-cache efficiency, not bigger matrices.

How do images enter the model?

An encoder maps patches into the same vector space as text tokens. The transformer just attends over a longer sequence.

Cost lever on vision calls?

Resolution and detail settings. A high-res image can cost more than a page of text.

What a span must carry

One trace per user request, with a span per step in the chain — rewrite, retrieve, rerank, generate, validate, tool call. Each span records:

  • Inputs and outputs, including the fully rendered prompt. Not the template — the actual string sent, or you cannot reproduce the failure.

  • Latency, token counts, and cost, so the expensive span is visible rather than inferred.
  • Retrieved chunk IDs and scores — the single most useful field, because it separates "we never found it" from "we found it and ignored it".
  • A correlation ID linking the trace to the user session and any downstream record written.

The case for not building an agent

Most tasks labelled "agentic" are a fixed sequence with a model in a few slots. A chain is testable, debuggable, costed in advance, and doesn't compound failure. Autonomy is what you spend when the path genuinely cannot be known in advance — and "we might want flexibility later" is not that. Being the person in the room who says this is worth more than being the person who has read every framework.

Search over reasoning: tree of thought

Chain of thought explores one line of reasoning. Tree of thought generates several candidate next steps, evaluates them, and expands the promising branches — search rather than a single path, with backtracking when a branch dies. Self-consistency is the cheap cousin: sample k independent chains and take the majority answer, with no search structure at all.

The honest assessment: tree search multiplies cost by the branching factor and only pays on problems with a verifiable intermediate signal — puzzles, code that compiles or doesn't, maths with checkable steps. On open-ended analysis there is usually nothing reliable to score branches with, so the search degenerates into expensive sampling. Reach for self-consistency first; reach for tree search when you have a verifier.

Making a loop survivable

  • Cap iterations — a hard ceiling, treated as both a reliability and a cost control.
  • Validate between steps so a bad observation doesn't enter context and poison every later iteration.
  • Checkpoint so a failure at step 8 resumes rather than restarts.
  • Manage context — summarise or drop old observations, or tokens grow superlinearly.
  • Gate irreversible actions on a human. Filing a report, freezing an account, sending anything external.

Interview point

"I'd start by asking whether it needs to be an agent at all — most 'agentic' tasks are a fixed workflow with a model in a few slots, and a chain is testable and costed in advance. When the path genuinely can't be known ahead, I'd use plan-and-execute over a raw ReAct loop in a regulated context, because the plan is a reviewable artefact you can gate before anything executes. A chain has steps you wrote; an agent decides its own. Use a chain whenever the procedure is known.

Reliability compounds and context grows superlinearly. The cap is both a correctness and a cost control.

When multi-agent actually earns its cost

  • Genuinely parallel subtasks — research five entities independently, then synthesise. Wall-clock wins because the work fans out.
  • Context isolation as a feature — one agent must not see what another does. In a bank, that can be an entitlement boundary rather than an efficiency choice.

MCP — the integration layer

The Model Context Protocol standardises how models reach tools and data. Instead of N bespoke integrations per application, a capability is exposed once as a server and any client can use it. Three primitives: tools (actions the model can call), resources (data it can read), and prompts (reusable templates).

The engineering value is boring and real: one integration surface, one place to enforce authorisation, one place to audit what was called. In a regulated environment that centralisation is the point — a tool server is a controllable perimeter in a way that per-app integrations are not.

The security note

An MCP server is a privileged surface. It executes actions on behalf of a model that may be reading attacker-influenced content. Authorisation belongs in the server against the user's identity, tool descriptions are attack surface too, and a third-party server is supply-chain risk with tool-call permissions attached.

Interview point

"I'd argue against multi-agent for most workloads, though not on token cost — context isolation can actually make that better. The rules

  • Stream everything. Perceived wait collapses from total response time to TTFT. It is the single highest-return change in the whole stack and costs almost nothing.
  • Show the chain, not a spinner. "Searching 4,200 documents… reranking… drafting" turns dead time into progress. In an agent, streaming the intermediate steps is what makes a 30-second run tolerable.

  • Make stopping easy. A stop button is a real feature — users abandon bad generations, and it saves tokens.
  • Design the failure states. "No relevant sources found" is a legitimate, useful answer. Give it a designed state rather than letting the model paper over it with prose.

A fluent answer reads as certain whether or not it should. Surface the evidence: inline citations, retrieval scores when they're weak, and an explicit low-confidence state. Agent memory is a different problem

Conversation memory is one thread of turns. Agent memory splits into three kinds, and conflating them is a common design error:

  • Working memory — the current loop's observations and intermediate results. Short-lived, and the thing that grows superlinearly if you never prune it.
  • Episodic memory — what happened in past runs. "I investigated this wallet last week and ruled out exchange behaviour." Usually a vector store over past traces, retrieved on demand.
  • Semantic memory — durable learned facts about the domain or the user, distinct from any single episode. In practice this is your knowledge base, and it belongs in retrieval rather than in a memory abstraction.

The design rule: working memory is state, episodic memory is retrieval, semantic memory is your corpus. Frameworks that present all three as one "memory" object hide the fact that they have completely different lifetimes, consistency requirements, and deletion semantics.

Memory is a compliance surface

Conversation history in a bank is retained data: it needs a retention policy, PII handling, and an answer to "delete everything about this customer." A summary that has absorbed a customer's details is now a derived record containing PII — and deletion has to reach it. Worse, in a multi-tenant assistant, memory is the easiest accidental cross-tenant leak: scope every memory store by tenant and user, and treat it as entitled data, not as cache.

Structured state is the underrated one

For an analyst tool, carrying {entities: [wallet_A], hypothesis: "layering", ruled_out: ["exchange"], pending: ["2-hop graph"]} beats a prose summary on every axis that matters: it survives compaction losslessly, it can be validated against a schema, it can be shown to the user for correction, and it can be written to a case record. Prose summaries are convenient; structured state is auditable.

The three findings that matter

  • Loss follows a power law in compute, parameters, and data. Not a cliff, not a plateau — a straight line on a log-log plot, over many orders of magnitude. This is what makes the spend predictable.
  • Chinchilla: for a fixed compute budget, the compute-optimal split is roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. The earlier generation of models was badly undertrained — too many parameters, too little data — which meant a smaller model trained longer beat a larger one.
  • Compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal. Chinchilla minimises training cost for a target loss. But you train once and serve forever, so it is rational to overtrain a smaller model far past 20:1 — paying more upfront to get a permanently cheaper, faster model. Modern small models are trained at 100:1 or beyond for exactly this reason.

The governing identity is worth memorising: C ≈ 6ND — training compute in FLOPs is about six times parameters times tokens. Two of those three numbers determine the third.

Where scaling laws stop being useful

They predict loss, not capability. Loss falls smoothly while specific abilities appear unevenly, so a curve that says "0.03 lower loss" tells you nothing about whether it can now do multi-step arithmetic. They also assume a fixed architecture, data distribution, and objective — change any of those and you are back to measuring. And the modern axis, test-time compute, is not in the original laws at all: quality that scales with reasoning length is a different curve entirely.

The objective

Take an anchor (a query), a positive (a document that answers it), and a set of negatives. InfoNCE loss is softmax over similarities: the positive should score highest.

L = −log exp(sim(a, p) / τ) / Σ exp(sim(a, x) / τ) for x in {p} ∪ negatives

Two things fall out. τ (temperature) controls how sharply the model is punished for near-misses — low τ makes it obsess over the hardest negative, high τ spreads the pressure. And the model only learns to distinguish things you actually put in the denominator.

The hard-negative problem

Negative typeWhat the model learns
Random"Fraud documents are not recipes." Trivially easy, gradient dies fast, and the resulting space cannot separate anything within a domain.
In-batchOther examples in the same batch are free negatives. This is why batch size is a quality parameter for embedding models — bigger batches mean more negatives per step.
Hard-minedRetrieve the top-k wrong documents with the current model and train against those. This is where real retrieval quality comes from — and it is iterative, since the definition of "hard" moves as the model improves.

False negatives poison hard mining

Mine the top-ranked wrong documents and some of them are not wrong — they are unlabelled correct answers. Training explicitly pushes them away, which actively degrades the model. Mitigations: a score threshold, a stronger model to filter candidates, or skipping the top few results entirely. This is the single subtlest failure in embedding training.

Bi-encoder, cross-encoder, and why BERT still matters

Embedding models and rerankers are almost always encoder-only architectures — the BERT lineage — not decoder-only generators. An encoder sees the whole sequence bidirectionally, which is exactly right when you want one representation of a fixed text and wrong when you want to generate the next token. That is the honest answer to "why do we still use BERT-style models": different objective, different architecture. Same transformer block underneath.

Two practical notes: Matryoshka training makes the first n dimensions independently useful, so you can truncate 1536 dims to 256 and keep most of the quality — a direct memory lever on your index. And instruction-prefixed embedding models expect a task prefix ("query: " vs "passage: "); getting that wrong silently costs you a chunk of retrieval quality.

Interview point

"Embedding models are trained contrastively — pull query and answer together, push negatives apart — so the quality ceiling is set by which negatives you sample. Building an eval set that means something

  • Size it for the effect you care about. Detecting a 5-point difference reliably needs hundreds of cases, not fifty. If you cannot afford that, say "inconclusive" rather than shipping on noise.
  • Use paired comparison. Running both versions on the same cases and comparing per-case outcomes is far more sensitive than comparing two independent rates — you remove case difficulty as a source of variance.
  • Stratify. Report per-category, not just overall. An average hides a category collapsing, which is the regression that actually hurts.
  • Hold out a set you never tune on. Iterate on a dev set; the test set is touched at decision time only. Otherwise you have overfitted your prompt to your eval — the same leakage problem as any model.
  • Freeze every fixed bug into the set. The eval grows from production failures. That is the loop that compounds.

Why a graph model at all

A tabular fraud model sees one wallet's features in isolation: age, velocity, volume. It cannot see that the wallet's counterparties are themselves suspicious, because that fact is not in the row. Guilt by association is the entire signal in laundering, and it lives in the edges.

Why a nonlinearity is not optional

Stack two linear layers and you get W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x — one linear layer wearing a costume. No matter how deep you go, a network of pure matrix multiplies can only draw a straight decision boundary. The nonlinearity is the entire reason depth buys anything.

The forward pass, in three lines

z = x @ W + b (batch, d_in) @ (d_in, d_out) → (batch, d_out) a = activation(z) elementwise, shape unchanged repeat a becomes the next layer's x

Batch dimension rides along untouched — which is why GPUs are so effective here. Processing 512 samples is one bigger matrix multiply, not 512 small ones, and the weights are read from memory once either way.

Why deep beats wide

A single wide hidden layer can in principle approximate any function. In practice it needs an impractical number of units, because it must memorise the function rather than build it. Depth composes: early layers learn simple features, later layers combine them. Every architecture in this bench — convolutional, recurrent, transformer — is a claim about which compositions are worth building in.

Interview point

"A layer is a matrix multiply plus a bias plus a nonlinearity, and without the nonlinearity a deep stack collapses to a single linear map. Depth matters because it composes features rather than memorising them. The practical thing I'd add is that the batch dimension is free — the weights get read from memory once regardless of batch size, which is why throughput and latency are different problems in a way people often conflate."

Quick-fire · night-before sheet
What shape is a layer's weight matrix?

(d_in × d_out), with a bias of length d_out. Parameters = d_in × d_out + d_out.

Why do you need a nonlinearity?

Without one, stacked linear layers collapse to a single linear map. Depth buys nothing.

Why is ReLU the default?

Constant gradient for positive inputs, so no saturation, and it's trivially cheap. Sigmoid and tanh saturate and kill gradients.

What is a dead ReLU?

A unit stuck outputting zero — gradient is zero there, so it never recovers. Leaky ReLU and GELU avoid it.

Deep or wide?

Deep. A wide shallow net can approximate anything in theory but must memorise; depth composes features.

Why do GPUs help so much?

The batch dimension turns many small operations into one large matmul, and weights are read from memory once regardless.

Optimisers and schedules

SGD with momentum accumulates velocity across steps and often generalises best, but needs tuning. Adam adapts a per-parameter step size from gradient statistics and converges fast with little tuning — the default. AdamW fixes how Adam applies weight decay, and is what modern models actually use.

The schedule matters as much as the optimiser: warmup ramps the learning rate from near zero over the first few hundred steps, because early gradients are large and noisy and a full-size step will wreck the initialisation. Then cosine decay anneals it toward zero so the model settles. Warmup plus cosine decay is the standard recipe, and skipping warmup on a transformer is a classic way to get a loss spike in the first hour.

Initialisation is not a detail

Initialise all weights to zero and every unit in a layer computes the same thing forever — no symmetry to break. Initialise too large and activations explode across depth; too small and they vanish. Xavier and He initialisation set the variance from the layer's fan-in so signal magnitude stays roughly constant through the stack. This is the same concern that layer normalisation and residual connections address at the architecture level.

Interview point

"Overfitting is a capacity-versus-data problem, and I read it off the gap between training and validation loss rather than the absolute numbers. The cheapest fixes in order are more data, early stopping, and weight decay. On the optimiser side I'd default to AdamW with warmup and cosine decay — warmup specifically because early gradients are large and noisy, and a full-size step there is a reliable way to spike the loss."

The two ideas

  • Local connectivity. A unit looks at a small patch, not the whole image. Nearby pixels are related; distant ones usually aren't.
  • Weight sharing. The same filter slides across every position. An edge detector learned in the corner works in the centre for free — translation invariance built into the architecture rather than learned from data.

Together these turn fifty million weights into a handful. A 3×3 filter is nine numbers plus a bias, regardless of image size.

The output size formula

out = floor((in + 2·padding − kernel) / stride) + 1

Worth memorising — it comes up in interviews and in every debugging session where the shapes don't line up. Pooling then downsamples (max-pool takes the strongest activation in each window), shrinking the map while widening what later layers see. Channels are the third dimension: a layer learns many filters in parallel, so a 3×3 convolution from 64 channels to 128 has 3×3×64×128 weights.

Why this matters in a transformer world

The comparison is the point, and it's the same trade from Track 2. A CNN's receptive field grows linearly with depth — a unit five layers in sees an 11-pixel window. Attention sees everything in one layer. That's why transformers won on language, where a pronoun can refer forty words back.

But CNNs remain the right answer where the inductive bias is true: locality and translation invariance are genuine properties of images and signals, and building them in means you need far less data than a vision transformer to reach the same place. Modern practice mixes both — convolutional stems feeding transformer blocks.

Interview point

"Convolution is weight sharing plus local connectivity — the same filter everywhere, so translation invariance is architectural rather than learned, and the parameter count stops depending on input size. The trade against attention is receptive field: a CNN's grows linearly with depth, attention covers everything in one layer. The mechanism

An RNN carries a hidden state forward: h_t = tanh(W·h_{t−1} + U·x_t + b). Every timestep applies the same weight matrix — weight sharing across time, exactly as a CNN shares across space.

The problem is in that recursion. Backpropagating through t steps multiplies by W roughly t times. Multiply a number slightly below 1 by itself forty times and it vanishes; slightly above 1 and it explodes. Gradient signal from step 40 back to step 1 dies before it arrives — so the model cannot learn long-range dependencies, no matter how much data you give it.

What LSTM and GRU did about it

An LSTM adds a separate cell state with an additive path and three gates — forget, input, output — that learn what to keep, what to write, and what to expose. Because the cell state is updated additively rather than by repeated matrix multiplication, gradients flow along it far better. GRU is a simplified two-gate version with comparable performance and fewer parameters.

Note the shape of the fix: an additive path that gradients travel along, bypassing the repeated transformation. That is the same idea as the residual connection in a transformer block. Different architecture, identical insight.

Why they lost anyway

Training parallelismNone. Step t needs step t−1, so a 2,000-token sequence is 2,000 sequential operations.All positions at once. This is the decisive one — it's what made trillion-token training possible.

That last row is why the idea isn't dead. State-space models (Mamba and relatives) are a modern return to recurrence with a linear-time inference path, engineered to also be parallelisable during training. If someone asks what might displace transformers, this is the honest answer.

The historical footnote worth knowing

Attention was invented for RNNs, not for transformers. Encoder-decoder translation models were bottlenecked by that single fixed-size vector, so attention was added to let the decoder look back at all encoder states. The 2017 paper's contribution was the title: you can drop the recurrence and keep only the attention. Knowing that ordering signals you've read the lineage rather than just the current state.

Interview point

"RNNs fail because backprop through time multiplies by the same matrix repeatedly — gradients vanish or explode, so long-range dependencies never get learned. LSTMs fix that with an additive cell path and gates, which is structurally the same trick as a residual connection. But the reason transformers won isn't accuracy, it's that recurrence can't be parallelised across the sequence, and that's what made training on trillions of tokens feasible."

Why do RNN gradients vanish?

Backprop through time multiplies by the same recurrent matrix at every step. Below 1 it decays to nothing, above 1 it explodes.

How does an LSTM fix it?

A separate cell state updated additively, with forget/input/output gates. Gradients flow along the additive path.

What does an LSTM share with a transformer block?

The additive skip path — gates in one, residual connections in the other. Same insight about gradient flow.

The decisive reason transformers won?

Parallel training across the sequence. RNNs are inherently sequential in time.

Where do RNNs still beat transformers?

Inference cost — O(n) time and constant memory per step, versus quadratic attention. State-space models revive this.

Was attention invented for transformers?

No — for RNN encoder-decoder translation, to escape the fixed-size context vector. The 2017 paper removed the recurrence.

ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC — the question you will be asked

ROC plots recall against false-positive rate. Its denominator for FPR is the huge negative class, so adding a thousand false positives barely moves it when you have a million clean transactions. ROC-AUC therefore looks flatteringly high on imbalanced problems.

PR plots precision against recall, and precision has FP in its denominator against TP only — so it is brutally sensitive to false positives exactly when positives are rare. On imbalanced data, report PR-AUC. Drag the fraud rate down in the simulator and watch ROC-AUC hold steady while PR-AUC collapses. That divergence is the whole argument, and having watched it happen is worth more than having read it.

The threshold is not the model's decision

A classifier outputs a score; 0.5 is an arbitrary default with no special status. Choosing the threshold is choosing the ratio of missed fraud to analyst hours, and that is a business decision with a cost attached. In practice you pick it from the operating capacity — "we can review 400 alerts a day" sets the threshold, and then you report the recall it buys. The simulator's alert-volume readout is that conversation.

The mistake that costs you a rewrite

Putting too much in state. Everything in the schema is serialised and checkpointed at every superstep — so stuffing raw retrieved documents or full tool payloads into state makes checkpoints enormous and writes slow. Keep references and summaries in state; keep the bulk in your object store or lakehouse and fetch it in the node that needs it. This is the same discipline as not putting blobs in a transactional table.

Where LangGraph sits now

LangChain and LangGraph both reached 1.0 in October 2025, and the relationship settled: LangGraph is the low-level runtime — state, nodes, edges, checkpointing, streaming — and LangChain is a thin, opinionated layer on top of it. You no longer choose between them; calling LangChain's create_agent hands you a compiled LangGraph graph underneath. The 1.0 releases came with a commitment to no breaking changes until 2.0, which is the first time this ecosystem has been safe to build a two-year system on.

Interview point

"LangGraph models an agent as a typed state machine — nodes are pure state transforms, fixed edges are control flow I wrote, and conditional edges are where the model gets to decide. That split is the whole value: the autonomous part is isolated to a router function I can unit-test, and everything else is deterministic. The design mistake I'd watch for is putting bulk data in state, because every superstep serialises the whole schema into a checkpoint."

What is a LangGraph node?

A function taking state and returning a partial state update. Testable in isolation, with no framework magic.

Edge vs conditional edge?

An edge is always taken. A conditional edge runs a router function over state to pick the next node — that's where autonomy is confined.

What is a reducer for?

Merging concurrent writes to the same state key. Without one, parallel nodes silently overwrite each other.

What is a superstep?

The execution unit: all scheduled nodes run, state merges, a checkpoint is written. Parallelism is several nodes in one superstep.

Why keep state small?

The whole schema is serialised at every superstep. Bulk payloads make checkpoints huge and writes slow.

Two operational details people get wrong

Run checkpointer.setup() in a migration, not at app start. It creates the tables. Doing it in the request path means every instance races to create schema it may lack permission for. Treat it as a CI/CD step like any other DDL.

Nodes must be idempotent. A resumed graph re-executes the interrupted node from its start, so a node that already sent an email before hitting the interrupt will send it again. Put side effects after the pause, or key them.

Why this is the argument for the framework

An agent waiting for approval consumes no compute — the state is in Postgres, and the process is gone. A reviewer can respond in ten minutes or two days. Building that yourself means an approval queue, a state store, a resume protocol, and a replay mechanism. LangChain's own 2026 report on agent engineering attributes more than 60% of production incidents to state management, which is a fair argument for not hand-rolling it.

Interview point

"Durable execution is the reason I'd take the framework. Checkpointing every superstep turns a mid-run failure from a restart into a resume, and the same persistence is what makes a human approval pause possible — the graph stops, state goes to Postgres, and nothing consumes compute until someone answers. The operational catch is idempotency: a resumed node re-runs from its start, so side effects belong after the interrupt or behind an idempotency key."

The middleware hooks

Six points around the agent loop: before_agent, before_model, wrap_model_call, wrap_tool_call, after_model, after_agent. Built-in middleware ships for human-in-the-loop, summarization, and PII redaction — which is to say, three of the things this bench spent whole modules on are now a line of config.

The framing worth borrowing: an agent is a model plus a harness, and create_agent is that harness — the minimal loop wrapping the model with its prompt, tools, and middleware. Everything you would otherwise scatter through your own request path becomes a named, ordered hook.

LCEL is not deprecated — the community keeps getting this wrong

The pipe style (prompt | llm | parser) was de-emphasised as the way to build agents, not removed. RunnableSequence, RunnableParallel, RunnableLambda, RunnableBranch and RunnablePassthrough are still the composition primitives, and they are still the right tool for a deterministic pipeline — a retrieval chain, a fan-out of independent calls, a formatting step. What changed is that you no longer express an agent loop that way. Use LCEL for chains, create_agent or a StateGraph for agents.

Interview point

"LangChain 1.0 was a reset, not a version bump — one agent entry point in create_agent, customisation through a middleware array instead of subclassing, and the legacy chain machinery moved to langchain-classic. The thing people still get wrong is assuming LCEL died with it; the pipe primitives are alive and correct for deterministic pipelines. The distinction I'd draw is chains versus agents: LCEL for a fixed sequence, a StateGraph the moment control flow depends on what a step returned."
